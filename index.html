The premise

Most ML contests hand you the data. This one hides it.

A conventional hackathon runs dataset → train model → submit accuracy. BLACKBOX AI runs the other way round. Your team is given access to a private machine learning system. You can send it inputs and read its outputs. Nothing else.

You do not know the training data, the preprocessing, the engineered features, the algorithm, or the decision thresholds. You have a fixed budget of queries, so every question you ask has to be worth asking.

The loop

Six steps, run over and over, under a shrinking budget. The teams that win are the ones whose fifth query is chosen because of what the first four returned.

  1. 01Query Spend one from a budget that never refills.
  2. 02Observe Record what actually came back, not what you expected.
  3. 03Hypothesise Name a mechanism inside the box that would explain it.
  4. 04Experiment Design the one query that separates two rival explanations.
  5. 05Infer Keep whichever explanation survives the evidence.
  6. 06Reconstruct Rebuild the hidden system from everything that survived.

Back to 01, with fewer queries left

A test of experimental reasoning under uncertainty — designing the experiment that separates two competing explanations, not memorising which library has which default.

Two days · six stages

The competition

Each stage hands you a hidden system and a query budget, and each one goes deeper than the last. Full rules and timings are given at the event briefing.

ROUND 0

Baseline

No elimination

A short round on ML fundamentals and experiment selection, to see where everyone is starting from. Nobody is cut.

ROUND 1

Observe

First cut

Your first real black box. Work out which features matter, where the thresholds sit, what is stable and what is not. Prove what you claim.

ROUND 2

Investigate

Day 2

Past surface behaviour into hidden preprocessing and feature interactions. By now several explanations fit what you have seen — find the experiment that tells them apart.

ROUND 3

Break

Day 2

Find where the system fails. Edge cases, unstable boundaries, systematic failure modes — with reproducible evidence for every weakness you claim.

ROUND 4

Reconstruct

Day 2

Build your own model that reproduces the hidden system's behaviour. Your queries are your training set. Scored against data you never see.

FINAL

The Unknown

Finalists

A fresh hidden system, identical for every finalist. Everything at once — then you explain your reasoning to the judging panel. Understanding has to back up the leaderboard.

06 — Questions

FAQ

Do I need to be an ML expert to enter?

No, but you should be comfortable with the basics — training a model, reading a scatter plot, reasoning about features. Round 0 is deliberately a baseline round that nobody is eliminated on. The later rounds reward careful thinking more than they reward knowing an exotic algorithm.

Are AI tools really allowed?

Yes, genuinely. We are not trying to run an AI-free competition — that is neither realistic nor useful. The difficulty comes from somewhere an assistant cannot reach: the hidden systems are private, built by us, and never exposed. An AI can help you analyse what the black box told you. It cannot tell you what the black box will say.

How big is a team?

Two or three students. One account per team, which all members can be logged into at once. The query budget is shared across the team, so coordinate — two people running the same sweep spends it twice.

What do I need to bring?

A laptop with Python and your usual ML libraries already installed and working — set this up before you arrive, not on the morning. Bring your charger. Details on connecting to the competition network are given in the Day 1 briefing and in the WhatsApp group.

What happens if I do not qualify after Round 1?

Round 1 results are published on the evening of Day 1 precisely so that you know before Day 2 begins. If your team does not qualify you are still welcome to attend, and you keep your participation certificate.

Is registration free?

Yes, entirely free. Sign up through the form to reserve your place and join the WhatsApp group — that group carries all official announcements. You create your actual team account at the venue on Day 1, and that is where your team name and login come from.

About IEEE

You already use IEEE's work every day

IEEE — the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers — is the world's largest technical professional organisation, with hundreds of thousands of members across more than 160 countries. Its roots go back to 1884; the modern body formed in 1963.

Most engineers meet IEEE long before they join it, usually without noticing:

IEEE 802.11Wi-Fi
IEEE 802.3Ethernet
IEEE 754Floating-point arithmetic
IEEE 1284Parallel interface

Every float your model trains on follows IEEE 754. Every packet in this room follows 802.11 or 802.3.

It is not only for computer science

IEEE's societies span power and energy, communications, robotics and automation, signal processing, biomedical engineering, aerospace, control systems, photonics and more — around forty of them. Whatever you end up building, there is almost certainly a society, a conference and a body of literature for it.

Our student branch sits with the IEEE Computer Society, which is where this event comes from — but membership is not limited to that.

What a student actually gets

Access to the literature

IEEE Xplore holds several million technical documents — papers, standards, conference proceedings. Being able to read the current work in your area, rather than a summary of it, changes how you approach a problem.

Conferences and publishing

Student rates for conferences, and a real route to presenting or publishing your own work while still an undergraduate.

A society in your field

Join the societies that match what you care about. Each brings its own publications, competitions and community — and they are not restricted to your degree title.

Things to run, not just attend

Student branches are run by students. Organising an event like this one is itself the benefit — it is experience you cannot get from coursework, and it is visible on a CV in a way attendance is not.

People outside your college

Local sections, regional events and a global membership. The useful part of a professional body is usually the person it puts you next to.

Scholarships and competitions

Funding, awards and student competitions run at branch, section and regional level throughout the year.

It does not stop at graduation

This is the part most students miss while they are still in college, and the part that ends up mattering most.

The library follows you out

Your college's journal access ends the day you leave. IEEE access does not. For anyone who keeps reading — and in this field you have to — that gap is the difference between staying current and guessing.

Sections are working engineers

A student branch is your peers. The local section is not: it is people ten and twenty years into the career you are starting. That is a different room to be in, and membership is how you get into it.

It travels

IEEE is recognised internationally. For graduate applications abroad, or a first job in another country, publications and a professional grade are legible to people who have never heard of your college.

There are grades above student

Member, then Senior Member, then Fellow — each earned, each meaning something specific to people in the field. It is a track you can actually be on rather than a badge you buy once.

Conferences are where hiring happens

Not the careers page. The person who reads your poster and asks a good question about it is often the person who remembers you a year later.

Standards are real work

The 802.11 in your laptop was written by people in working groups anyone can participate in. Contributing to a standard is engineering with a longer half life than most products.

What people get wrong about it

“It's just a subscription.”

A subscription is something you pay for and then receive. This is the other way round: almost everything worth having from it — the society you join, the people you meet, the paper you present, the event you run — happens because you did something, not because you paid. The fee buys you the door, not the room.

“It's only for ECE and EEE.”

The Computer Society is one of its largest, and its societies cover robotics, biomedical, aerospace, power, signal processing and more. Your branch is what you make of it, not what your degree is called.

“It's only useful if you want to publish papers.”

Publishing is one route. Reading the current work in your area, joining a society, running events, and meeting people outside your college are the parts most students actually use.

“It's just a line on a CV.”

It is, if you only pay the fee. What is worth something is what you did with it — organised, presented, competed, built. That is visible; membership alone is not.

“You have to be a final-year topper.”

No. First years can join, and branches usually need the people who show up more than they need the people with the highest marks.

“Nothing really happens at our branch.”

A branch is only as active as the students running it — there is nobody else to do it. This event was built by the branch, in our own college, for our own students. That is what happens when people show up.

Student membership is discounted, and joining runs through ieee.org. If you want to know what it is like in practice, ask any of the organisers on the day — they are all student members, and this event is what a branch does.
